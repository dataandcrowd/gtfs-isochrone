# Discussion (draft, urban-only sample as main, four topics)

## 5.1 No-alternative payers and the case for public transport investment

The motorway-corridor scenarios produce a striking incidence figure. Under 3b and 3c, approximately three quarters of the charged commuter set falls into the no-alternative class, that is, residents who would pay the charge with public transport accessibility below the regional Q75 cut-point of 85,782 reachable jobs within 45 minutes. In commuter terms this is roughly 180,000 of the 226,000 charged car commuters under 3b, and a similar share under 3c. The implication for sequencing is direct. If the policy goal is mode shift, charging a population whose alternative does not exist cannot deliver mode shift. It delivers cost without choice.

The implication for investment is also direct. The 77 SA2s of the LH burden cluster (low 45-minute accessibility, high deprivation neighbours) are not 77 scattered cases. They form two connected corridors, the outer south (Manurewa, Papakura, Takanini, Wiri outer, Weymouth) and the outer west (Henderson, Massey, Ranui, Te Atatū South-North). Together these two clusters contain around 200,000 residents in the most deprived NZDep deciles. A targeted public transport upgrade programme on the southern rail extension, the southern motorway bus corridor, and the western rail to Henderson and beyond would lift a large share of these SA2s above the Q75 cut-point before any charge is applied. The pre-specified Q75 threshold makes the audit numerically tractable. For each candidate intervention, the analyst can ask whether the post-upgrade access_45min for the affected SA2s would exceed 85,782 jobs, and reclassify accordingly. We treat this corridor audit as the most directly useful follow-on analysis to the present paper.

## 5.2 Why the motorway corridor scenarios are regressive

The scenario-level Concentration Index of 45-minute accessibility against deprivation, restricted to the charged SA2 subset, is -0.216 for 3b and -0.155 for 3c on the urban sample. Both values sit clearly in the regressive range. The mechanism is geographical. The motorway corridor footprints cut almost exclusively through outer-south and outer-west Auckland, the band where low accessibility and high deprivation already coincide before any pricing is applied. The charged subset under 3b and 3c is therefore drawn from the part of the city where the access-deprivation gradient is steepest, and the per-SA2 statistic reflects that gradient.

Three points reinforce the substantive reading. First, the result is robust to the population-weighted Concentration Index, which gives values of -0.211 and -0.152 respectively. Second, the result is robust to dropping rural SA2s under the urban filter, which slightly strengthens the regressive sign because the two rural charged SA2s removed from 3b and 3c are not in the deprived end of the gradient. Third, the contrast with the CBD-cordon and isthmus scenarios is clean. 1a (Concentration Index -0.005), 1c (+0.033), and 2c (+0.026) sit at or near zero, indicating that those scenarios do not load disproportionately on the deprived end of the gradient and are not equity-active in the regressive sense.

A subtlety in the 3b versus 3c comparison deserves explicit treatment. 3c is 3b plus the central business district, and adds a further 15 SA2s to the charged set. The Concentration Index for 3c is less negative (-0.155) than for 3b (-0.216). The naive reading would be that adding the central business district makes the scheme less regressive. It does not. The same 340,908 residents would face the charge with no viable 45-minute public transport alternative under both scenarios. The 15 added city centre SA2s sit well above the Q75 cut-point and span a wide range of deprivation scores, which introduces high-access points across the deprivation axis of the charged subset and dilutes the regressive gradient. The Concentration Index narrows, but the underlying inequity in scale is unchanged. The corollary is that the Concentration Index value should be read alongside the incidence count and not in isolation.

## 5.3 Land-use co-investment and the longer planning horizon

The present analysis takes the residential pattern as given. The 77 SA2s of the LH burden cluster are large because Auckland's residential geography places a substantial share of its lower-income population in the outer south and outer west, while the commercial and employment density remains concentrated in the central isthmus. The mismatch between where people live and where jobs are is the structural condition that the Concentration Index detects.

This points to a complementary policy lever that this paper does not evaluate but cannot ignore. The National Policy Statement on Urban Development 2020 and the related Plan Change 78 framework allow significant intensification within walking distance of frequent public transport stops in Auckland. Recent work on the Auckland Unitary Plan and the more recent intensification provisions (the PC120 hearing process and its successor instruments) sets the upzoning envelope around the rail and Northern Busway corridors. If the next decade of housing supply concentrates in those upzoned catchments, the LH burden cluster shrinks for two reasons at once. Some residents in the present-day outer south and outer west move toward intensifying inner corridors, and the per-SA2 access_45min for the remaining outer-suburban residents rises as service frequency follows population. The Time of Use charge then operates on a different city, in which charged residents have an alternative more often than not.

This is not an argument that pricing must wait for intensification, nor an argument that intensification is sufficient on its own. It is an argument that the equity outcome of any Time of Use scheme depends on the joint trajectory of pricing, public transport investment, and residential intensification, and that policy modelling should not treat the residential pattern as static. A useful next step is to repeat the present analysis under projected residential distributions for the 2030 and 2040 horizons, using the upzoned-catchment dwelling capacity as the displacement signal.

## 5.4 Limitations of the urban-only sample

The headline figures in Sections 4 and 5 of this paper use the urban-only sample, defined as the 562 SA2s with greater than 50 per cent area overlap with a Stats NZ IUR2025 class 11 to 14 polygon (Major, Large, Medium, or Small urban area). The all-region sample of 619 SA2s is reported in parallel as a sensitivity check. The urban restriction matters for two reasons.

First, the inverted-U accessibility-deprivation surface reported by earlier drafts of this paper is largely a contrast between affluent rural-fringe SA2s in deciles 1 and 2 (Coatesville, Riverhead, Kumeu, Maraetai, Whangaparaoa rural pockets) and deprived outer-suburban SA2s in deciles 9 and 10. Restricting to urban SA2s removes the rural-fringe tail, and the resulting accessibility-deprivation curve is close to flat with a baseline Concentration Index near zero (-0.001 unweighted, -0.011 population-weighted). The substantive within-Auckland inequity remains visible in the D8 to D10 subgroup Concentration Index of -0.13 to -0.17, but the inverted-U narrative loses its overall framing under the urban filter and becomes a within-region observation about the affluent rural fringe.

Second, the urban-only sample sets aside the question of public transport poverty in rural and peri-urban Auckland. The 57 SA2s excluded under the filter include Snells Beach, Wellsford, Helensville, Waiuku, Pukekohe rural, the outer Whangaparaoa, parts of Beachlands-Pine Harbour, Waiheke West, and similar settlements. By definition these areas have lower public transport accessibility than the urban core, and their residents are not represented in our headline equity statistic. The Time of Use scheme does not currently propose to charge them either, since the shortlisted scenarios sit entirely within or close to the urban service area, so the absence of these residents from the equity statistic is internally consistent with the policy scope. However, future scheme variants or extensions toward Pukekohe or the Hibiscus Coast would require the rural-included sample to be reinstated.

A further limitation common to both samples is the proxy assumption. The framework treats 45-minute public transport accessibility at the population-weighted SA2 origin as a proxy for the realistic availability of a public transport alternative at the journey-to-work level. It is not a behavioural prediction of mode shift. Households in well-served SA2s may continue to drive because of trip purpose, family logistics, or service-level perceptions not captured by the routing model, and households in poorly-served SA2s may find a way to switch because of charge magnitude relative to household income, or because of service-level changes after the rollout. The natural empirical check on this proxy assumption is to compare the "pays with alternative" classification against observed Auckland Transport HOP patronage growth on recently service-uplifted corridors since 2023. We do not undertake that validation in this paper and defer it to a companion methodological note.
